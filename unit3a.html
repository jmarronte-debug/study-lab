The big picture

One sentence for the whole unit: living things have to pay a fee every time they move energy around, enzymes control how fast that energy gets used, and photosynthesis is how a plant makes its own supply from sunlight.

Every topic in Unit 3a answers one of four questions:

  1. What are the rules energy has to follow? Thermodynamics and ATP (3.3).
  2. What speeds a reaction up, in a cell-safe way? Enzyme structure and catalysis (3.1–3.2).
  3. What slows an enzyme down or shuts it off on purpose? Environment, inhibitors, and feedback (3.2).
  4. Where does a plant's usable energy actually come from? Photosynthesis: the light reactions and the Calvin cycle (3.5).

Scope check: this guide stops before cellular respiration (glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, fermentation) and before fitness/3.6–3.7. Those are Unit 3b. If a question mentions NADH, the mitochondrial matrix, or pyruvate, it's next unit — don't let it creep in here.

IDEA 1 · 3.3

Energy follows two rules, and every conversion costs something.

In plain English: Energy can't be created or destroyed — it only changes form (1st Law). But every time it changes form, some of it leaks away as heat, so the universe gets a little messier each time (2nd Law: entropy increases). Neither law is about cells being wasteful. It's just the toll every conversion charges.

A number that makes the 2nd Law concrete

Building about 1 kg of animal body takes roughly 10 kg of food broken down along the way. That's not inefficiency to feel bad about — it's the 2nd Law showing up in real life. Most of that energy isn't "wasted," it's converted to heat at every single step between the food and the new tissue, and that heat has to go somewhere (which is part of why animals are warm).

Why living systems take in more energy than they give off

This follows directly from the 2nd Law, and it's one of her exact outline questions: every single energy conversion loses some usable energy as heat along the way. If an organism only took in exactly as much energy as it needed to do its work, it would come up short every time, because some of that intake is always lost as heat before it ever becomes useful work or new tissue. So a living system has to take in more energy than it releases as work and growth — the surplus is what pays the unavoidable heat "toll" of the 2nd Law. Taking in less than that, or exactly that, isn't sustainable; organisms that couldn't take in enough surplus energy wouldn't survive.

Why energy gets released in small, controlled steps — not all at once

Another exact outline question: why do living systems break energy release into a series of small steps (like the many steps of cellular respiration) instead of releasing it all in one burst? Two reasons tie together. First, a single huge release of energy would come out mostly as an uncontrolled burst of heat — enough to damage or kill the cell — rather than usable free energy. Second, releasing it step by step lets the cell actually capture some of that energy at each individual step (often by making ATP at that step), instead of losing almost all of it to heat in one shot. Controlled, stepwise release is what makes the energy usable instead of just destructive.

Potential, kinetic, and free energy

TermMeans
Potential energyStored energy — for example, in a chemical bond
Kinetic energyThe energy of motion
Free energyThe energy that's actually available to do cellular work, after accounting for the energy lost as heat

"Energy" and "free energy" aren't the same thing. A reaction can release a lot of energy overall, but only the free energy portion is usable by the cell. That distinction is exactly why the next idea (ATP) exists.

Exergonic vs. endergonic, anabolism vs. catabolism

ExergonicEndergonic
Free energyReleases free energyRequires a net input of free energy
ExampleBreaking down glucose (catabolism)Building glucose in photosynthesis (anabolism)
Molecule sizeLarge → small (catabolism)Small → large (anabolism)
ANCHOR IT
Catabolism breaks big things down and releases energy, so it's exergonic. Anabolism builds small things up and costs energy, so it's endergonic. Catabolism tears apart; anabolism assembles.
What this topic is NOT: the teacher's own slides leave the Gibbs free energy equation (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS) off the AP exam — this is a concept-only topic here. If a question asks you to calculate ΔG from enthalpy and entropy, that's not what you'll see; you need to be able to say whether a reaction is exergonic or endergonic and why, not plug numbers into that formula.

IDEA 2 · 3.3–3.4

ATP: small, safe packets instead of one giant release.

In plain English: A cell could, in theory, release all of glucose's energy in one burst — but that would be like trying to light a candle with a stick of dynamite. Instead, cells store and spend energy in small, immediately usable packets called ATP. Breaking down ATP (hydrolysis) is exergonic, and the cell spends that released energy on jobs that need it.

ATP structure

Adenine (a nitrogenous base) + ribose (a 5-carbon sugar) + three phosphate groups in a row. Swap out two of those phosphates and you get ADP (adenosine diphosphate) — the "spent" form.

Why ATP instead of just using food energy directly

Food (glucose) holds a huge amount of energy, released all at once if you just burned it. ATP breaks that energy into small, immediately usable packets the cell can spend exactly where and when it's needed, without a dangerous burst of heat in one spot.

Correct this exact misconception — the teacher flags it directly: it is not true that "breaking the phosphate bond releases energy." Breaking any bond actually costs energy; that's true of every bond, always. ATP hydrolysis is exergonic for a different reason: the products (ADP + a free phosphate) are more stable and lower-energy than ATP was. Part of why: ATP's three phosphates are all negatively charged and crowded close together, repelling each other — like a coiled spring. Separating them and letting them spread out releases that stored repulsion energy. It's the products being more stable, not the bond-breaking itself, that makes the overall reaction exergonic.

Energy coupling

Cells pair up the exergonic hydrolysis of ATP with an endergonic reaction elsewhere that needs energy — building a molecule, pumping an ion, moving something. Unit 2 link: this is exactly what the sodium-potassium pump does — it couples ATP hydrolysis to pumping Na⁺ and K⁺ against their gradients. And just like Idea 1 said, heat is released at every one of these coupled steps — the 2nd Law never takes a day off.

What coupling actually buys the cell (another exact outline question — what does this allow that wouldn't be possible otherwise?): an endergonic reaction on its own won't run — it needs a net input of free energy, and left alone it just doesn't happen spontaneously. Coupling it to ATP hydrolysis (which IS spontaneous/exergonic) supplies that missing energy, so the cell can force reactions to run that could never run by themselves. For the pairing to actually work, the exergonic reaction has to release more free energy than the endergonic reaction needs — the ATP hydrolysis step has to be the bigger number. The extra, left-over energy is what gets lost as heat (2nd Law again); if the exergonic side released exactly the same amount or less, there'd be nothing driving the endergonic side forward.

IDEA 3 · 3.1

Enzymes are catalysts with a very specific shape.

In plain English: A catalyst speeds a reaction up without being used up and without changing whether the reaction can happen — only how fast it happens. Enzymes are biology's catalysts, and they work because their shape (and charge) fits a specific substrate.

Activation energy: the hill a reaction has to climb

Before a reaction can happen, the substrate has to pass through an unstable, high-energy in-between state. The energy needed to get there is the activation energy (Eₐ). An enzyme lowers that hill — it does not change the starting energy level of the substrate or the ending energy level of the product. E + S → ES → E + P.

Specificity: shape AND charge, not shape alone

Tripping point: "the substrate just has to be the right shape" is an incomplete answer. Both the shape and the charge of the substrate have to match the active site. A substrate can be the right shape and still fail to bind if its charges don't line up with the active site's.

Lock-and-key vs. induced fit

ModelIdea
Lock-and-key (older)The enzyme is a rigid, passive shape that the substrate simply fits into, like a key into a lock
Induced fit (the real model)The enzyme flexes slightly as the substrate binds, improving the fit as it goes

Three ways enzymes actually speed a reaction up

  • Stretching the substrate toward its unstable, in-between shape (lowering Eₐ directly)
  • Holding two substrates in exactly the right position next to each other, so they don't have to collide randomly
  • Directly lending a chemical group to help the reaction along

Cofactors and coenzymes

Many enzymes need help: cofactors are inorganic helpers (metal ions, like Mg²⁺ or Zn²⁺); coenzymes are organic helpers, often made from vitamins. The protein part alone is called the apoenzyme; the protein plus its cofactor/coenzyme together is the working holoenzyme.

REMEMBER
Cofactor = inorganic (think: metal). Coenzyme = organic (think: vitamin-derived). Apo = incomplete; holo = whole and working.

IDEA 4 · 3.2

Two shapes of curve students mix up, and why they're shaped that way.

In plain English: Temperature and pH both affect enzyme rate, but their graphs look different for a mechanical reason, not a random one. Learn the why and you'll never mix them up again.

Temperature vs. rate: asymmetric, with a steep cliff

Rate rises slowly up to the enzyme's optimum temperature, then crashes steeply past it. Why asymmetric? Because heat doesn't gently loosen a protein's shape as it rises — it builds up until it suddenly breaks the bonds holding the 3° and 4° structure together, and that's permanent: denaturation.

pH vs. rate: symmetric

Rate falls off at roughly the same rate whether the pH goes too acidic or too basic in either direction — a smooth curve centered on the enzyme's optimum pH, not a cliff.

Real examples — would pepsin work in the intestine?

Pepsin (stomach) works best around pH 1.5–2. Trypsin and lipase (intestine) work best around pH 8. Would pepsin work in the intestine? No — the wrong pH would denature it. Each enzyme's optimum matches the environment it actually works in.

What denaturation actually is, mechanically

The H-bonds and ionic interactions holding the enzyme's 3° (or 4°) structure together break. The active site loses its shape. The substrate no longer fits. Unit 1 link: this is the exact same 3°/4° structure — R-group interactions — you already studied for proteins in general.

"As the temperature increases beyond the optimum, the enzyme reaction rate decreases. This is because the H-bonds and ionic bonds stabilizing the 3° and 4° structure in the enzyme will break and the enzyme will begin to denature. This denaturation will disrupt the shape of the active site and reduce the interaction of the enzyme with the substrate. The result is a decrease in the catalytic function of the enzyme and a decrease in the reaction rate."

That's a model of what a complete answer looks like: it names the actual bonds, the actual structure level, and walks the mechanism all the way through to rate — not just "it denatures."

Substrate concentration vs. enzyme concentration: two more curves that differ for a reason

Rate vs. Δ[substrate] (enzyme fixed)Rate vs. Δ[enzyme] (substrate in excess)
ShapeRises, then plateausRises in a straight line, no plateau
WhyOnce every active site is occupied, adding more substrate can't speed things up — saturationSubstrate is never the limiting factor, so more enzyme just keeps making more product
Tripping point: don't mix these two curves up with temperature/pH. Those two denature or disrupt the enzyme; these two are about running out of active sites (or not).

Salt (ionic) concentration — the fifth factor, and why it works like pH

Her outline lists salt concentration right alongside temperature and pH as something that affects enzyme structure and function, so don't skip it. The mechanism is close cousin to pH, not temperature: an enzyme's 3° and 4° structure is held together partly by ionic bonds and interactions between charged R-groups. A very high or very low salt (ion) concentration disrupts those same charge interactions — too many stray ions in solution "shield" or interfere with the charged groups that are supposed to attract each other, which can distort the active site the same way an extreme pH does. Too little salt can be a problem too: some enzymes actually need a minimum ion concentration nearby to hold their shape correctly. Either direction — too much or too little — can reduce or stop catalytic function.

IDEA 5 · 3.2

Inhibitors shut enzymes off on purpose; feedback is the cell's thermostat.

In plain English: Sometimes a cell needs to slow or stop an enzyme deliberately, not by accident (like denaturation). Inhibitors do that in two different spots, and feedback inhibition is how a whole pathway polices itself.

Competitive vs. noncompetitive (allosteric) inhibitors

TypeWhere it bindsEffect
CompetitiveThe active site itselfPhysically blocks the substrate — "if it fits, it sits" there instead of the real substrate
Noncompetitive / allostericSomewhere else on the enzymeChanges the enzyme's shape so the active site no longer fits the substrate

Allosteric activation is the mirror image of noncompetitive inhibition: binding somewhere else on the enzyme improves the active site's fit instead of ruining it.

Feedback inhibition: the cell's built-in thermostat

In a multi-step pathway, the end product loops back and shuts off (usually) the very first enzyme in that same pathway. The cell stops making something it already has plenty of — the same logic as a thermostat shutting off the heat once the room's warm enough.

IDEA 6 · 3.5

Sunlight can't be stored directly — so plants convert it into sugar.

In plain English: Light energy can't just sit in a cell waiting to be used, and it can't be shipped to another part of the plant. To actually store and transport that energy, it has to be converted into something stable: glucose. That's the whole point of photosynthesis.

About the Crash Course Biology #28 homework: this guide doesn't replicate that worksheet's exact fill-in-the-blank questions — still watch the actual 13-minute video and fill it in for the graded homework. But everything the worksheet asks (producers/consumers, why photosynthesis and respiration are complementary, where ATP and NADPH come from, what the Calvin cycle's 3-carbon product is) is covered here in Ideas 6–8, so this guide is a good way to check your worksheet answers afterward.

The real equation (not the simplified one)

Light + 6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O

Tripping point: most textbooks show a simplified version with only 6H₂O on each side and cancel them out — that hides where the extra 6 waters actually go. 12 waters go in; only 6 come out (a net 6 are consumed). The 12 that go in get split apart in the light reactions. The 6 that come out are made fresh during the Calvin cycle. They are not the same water molecules passing straight through.

Photosynthesis is a redox reaction

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). CO₂ is reduced (gains electrons, becomes glucose). H₂O is oxidized (loses electrons, becomes O₂).

Chloroplast structure, the quick tour

PartWhat it is
Outer / inner membraneThe double membrane around the whole organelle (endosymbiotic evidence — Unit 2)
StromaThe fluid inside the inner membrane, where the Calvin cycle happens
ThylakoidsFlattened membrane sacs, stacked into grana, where the light reactions happen
Thylakoid space (lumen)The compartment inside the thylakoid membrane

Pigments: absorption spectrum vs. action spectrum

Chlorophyll absorbs mostly blue and red light and reflects green — that's why plants look green. The absorption spectrum (what wavelengths a pigment actually soaks up, measured directly) is close to, but not identical to, the action spectrum (what wavelengths actually drive the most photosynthesis, measured by oxygen output). The two differ slightly because of accessory pigments that absorb other wavelengths and pass that energy along.

HISTORY
Engelmann's experiment: a strip of algae was lit through a prism (splitting light into its colors) alongside light-loving bacteria. The bacteria clustered most densely exactly where chlorophyll absorbs best — the first real evidence tying specific wavelengths of light to photosynthesis.

IDEA 7 · 3.5

The light reactions: follow one electron.

In plain English: Follow a single electron's journey and the whole light-reaction diagram makes sense. A photon excites it, it gets passed down a chain while that energy builds a pressurized pocket of H⁺, and that pressure is what actually makes ATP.

The path, step by step

  1. A photon hits Photosystem II (PSII), exciting an electron at its reaction center, P680.
  2. That electron is handed down a chain of proteins, the electron transport chain (ETC). The energy released along the way pumps H⁺ into the thylakoid space, building up a crowded, acidic pocket.
  3. Those H⁺ flow back out through ATP synthase — like water through a turbine — and that flow makes ATP. This whole process is chemiosmosis.
  4. Meanwhile, the electron reaches Photosystem I (PSI), gets re-excited by another photon at its reaction center, P700, and is handed to NADP⁺, turning it into NADPH.
  5. Both ATP and NADPH head to the Calvin cycle.

Where PSII's missing electrons come from: water molecules are split apart (photolysis) to replace them — and that splitting is also where the O₂ byproduct comes from.

This whole path — water → PSII → ETC → PSI → NADPH — is non-cyclic electron flow.

Cyclic electron flow: when the cell needs more ATP than NADPH

Sometimes a cell already has plenty of NADPH but still needs more ATP. The electron leaving PSI gets rerouted back into the ETC instead of going to NADP⁺ — making more ATP but no new NADPH. This exists because the Calvin cycle needs more ATP than NADPH (not a 1:1 ratio) — cyclic flow lets the cell dial that ratio up or down as needed.

ORDER
PSII (P680) → ETC (pumps H⁺) → ATP synthase (ATP) → PSI (P700) → NADPH. Water is split to refill PSII's electrons; that's where O₂ comes from.

IDEA 8 · 3.5

The Calvin cycle has to turn around twice to make one glucose.

In plain English: The Calvin cycle spends the ATP and NADPH the light reactions made, to build sugar out of CO₂. It happens in the stroma, in three phases, and it has to run around more than once before a full glucose molecule exists.

Three phases

  1. Carbon fixation. The enzyme Rubisco grabs 3 CO₂ molecules, one at a time, and attaches each to RuBP (a 5-carbon sugar), producing 6 molecules of 3-PGA (a 3-carbon compound).
  2. Reduction. Those 6 PGA molecules each get a phosphate from ATP, then electrons/hydrogens from NADPH, turning into 6 G3P (also called PGAL) — a 3-carbon sugar (a carbohydrate, specifically a triose phosphate; another exact outline question is "what type of macromolecule is G3P," and that's the answer). This is the step where the Calvin cycle actually spends the ATP and NADPH the light reactions made. One of those 6 G3P leaves the cycle — the actual payoff, headed toward glucose or whatever else the plant needs. Combine two G3P molecules (6 carbons total) and you get one glucose.
  3. Regeneration. The other 5 G3P molecules get rearranged, using a bit more ATP, back into 3 RuBP, so the cycle can run again.
Why it takes two turns: only 1 G3P comes out per turn, but it takes 2 G3P to build 1 glucose. So the Calvin cycle has to turn around twice to make one glucose's worth of material. The real totals for 1 glucose: 6 CO₂, 12 NADPH, 18 ATP.
A subtle but real trap: the 3 carbon atoms that leave as G3P in one turn are not necessarily the exact same 3 atoms that entered as CO₂ that same turn. They're mixed into a shared pool of intermediates. Don't picture a straight line "this CO₂ becomes this G3P" — the cycle pools and redistributes carbons.

IDEA 9 · 3.1–3.2, 3.5

Reading real data: catalase and the AMY1 gene.

In plain English: Two real datasets you'll see on the test: one shows an enzyme's temperature optimum in action, the other shows natural selection acting on an enzyme-related gene in actual human history.

The catalase / temperature experiment

Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂). Measuring how much O₂ is produced at different temperatures — with an O₂ gas sensor, after exactly 30 seconds of reaction time at each temperature — reveals the enzyme's temperature curve directly. This is the real data from the class's enzyme-graphing practice FRQ:

Temp (°C)O₂ gas produced (%) ± 2SE
49.7 ± 0.5
2113.7 ± 0.7
3423.8 ± 1.2
516.0 ± 1.0

Activity rises from 4°C up through 34°C, then crashes to 6.0% at 51°C. That's consistent with a real-world optimum somewhere in the low-to-mid 30s°C, with denaturation well underway before 51°C — exactly the asymmetric curve from Idea 4. (One worked number: the rate of O₂ production at 34°C is 23.8% ÷ 30 sec ≈ 0.79% O₂ per second — always finish a calculation like this to a decimal, never leave it as a fraction.)

Connect it to the teacher's graphing rules

  • Continuous data → a line graph, with the line actually drawn in connecting the points.
  • Label both axes with a title and units.
  • Title the graph "The effect of [independent variable] on [dependent variable]."
  • Never extrapolate past the real data you collected.
  • Calculate error bars before you scale the axes, so the scale actually accommodates them.
  • No color — tests are scanned in grayscale, so color-coding earns nothing (and can cost clarity).

The AMY1 / salivary amylase story

Humans vary from 2 to 15 copies of the AMY1 gene. More copies → more amylase enzyme in saliva → better starch digestion. Populations with a long history of starch-rich diets (farmers) tend to carry more AMY1 copies on average than populations with a long history of low-starch, protein-rich diets (hunter-gatherers). This is a real, documented example of natural selection acting on an enzyme-related gene within human history — a good bridge from Unit 3 (enzymes) into evolution.

Word pairs (29 pairs — enzymes, energy, and photosynthesis only)

Word-pair questions test whether she can tell two related things apart. The winning answer always has two halves: how they're alike, then the one difference that matters, stated for both. Tap a pair only after saying both halves out loud. (Cellular respiration terms like glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, NADH, and the matrix are Unit 3b — not here.)

1st Law of Thermodynamics & 2nd Law
Alike: Both describe rules every energy conversion must follow
Different: 1st Law: energy is never created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another. 2nd Law: every conversion loses some energy as heat, so disorder (entropy) in the universe increases.
Idea 1
Anabolism & catabolism
Alike: Both are categories of metabolic reactions
Different: Anabolism builds small molecules into large ones and costs energy (endergonic). Catabolism breaks large molecules into small ones and releases energy (exergonic).
Idea 1
Exergonic & endergonic
Alike: Both describe the free-energy change of a reaction
Different: Exergonic reactions release free energy (products have less than reactants); endergonic reactions require a net input of free energy (products have more than reactants).
Idea 1
Energy & free energy
Alike: Both measure a system's capacity to do something
Different: "Energy" is the total amount involved in a conversion. "Free energy" is only the portion actually available to do cellular work, after some is lost as heat.
Idea 1
Open system & closed system
Alike: Both describe whether energy/matter can be exchanged with the surroundings
Different: An open system exchanges energy (and often matter) with its surroundings — living things are open systems. A closed system does not exchange matter with its surroundings (though it may still exchange energy).
Idea 1
ATP & ADP
Alike: Both are adenine + ribose + phosphate groups
Different: ATP has three phosphates and is the "charged," higher-energy form. ADP has two phosphates and is the "spent," lower-energy form, after hydrolysis releases a phosphate.
Idea 2
Energy of activation & free energy
Alike: Both are energy quantities involved in a reaction
Different: Activation energy (Eₐ) is the "hill" that must be climbed before a reaction can start, which enzymes lower. Free energy is the difference between the reactants' and products' energy, which enzymes do NOT change.
Idea 3
Lock-and-key & induced fit
Alike: Both are models for how an enzyme's active site binds its substrate
Different: Lock-and-key treats the active site as a rigid shape the substrate simply fits into. Induced fit (the accurate model) has the enzyme flex slightly as the substrate binds, improving the fit.
Idea 3
Competitive inhibitor & noncompetitive inhibitor
Alike: Both are molecules that reduce enzyme activity by binding to the enzyme
Different: Competitive binds directly in the active site, physically blocking the substrate. Noncompetitive (allosteric) binds elsewhere, changing the enzyme's shape so the active site no longer fits the substrate.
Idea 5
Cofactor & coenzyme
Alike: Both are non-protein helpers an enzyme needs to function (together with the protein, called a holoenzyme)
Different: Cofactors are inorganic (metal ions). Coenzymes are organic, often vitamin-derived.
Idea 3
Graph of rate vs. Δ[substrate] (fixed [enzyme]) & graph of rate vs. Δ[enzyme] (excess substrate)
Alike: Both show how reaction rate changes as you increase the amount of one thing
Different: Rate vs. substrate (enzyme fixed) rises then plateaus once every active site is saturated. Rate vs. enzyme (substrate in excess) rises as a straight line, with no plateau, because substrate is never the limiting factor.
Idea 4
Graph of rate vs. ΔpH & graph of rate vs. Δtemperature
Alike: Both show an optimum where rate peaks, with rate falling off on either side
Different: The pH curve is roughly symmetric — rate falls off at a similar pace too acidic or too basic. The temperature curve is asymmetric — a slow rise to the optimum, then a steep, cliff-like crash past it, because heat denatures the enzyme fast and permanently.
Idea 4
Feedback inhibition & allosteric regulation
Alike: Both control enzyme activity by binding somewhere other than the active site
Different: Feedback inhibition is specifically the end product of a pathway shutting off (usually) the first enzyme in that same pathway. Allosteric regulation is the broader mechanism — binding at a non-active site to either inhibit OR activate an enzyme — that feedback inhibition is one example of.
Idea 5
Light-dependent reaction & light-independent reaction
Alike: Both are stages of photosynthesis, both happen in the chloroplast
Different: Light-dependent (light reactions) directly require light and happen in the thylakoid membrane, producing ATP and NADPH and releasing O₂. Light-independent (Calvin cycle) doesn't directly need light — it uses the ATP and NADPH already made — and happens in the stroma.
Idea 6–8
Light-independent reaction & Calvin cycle
Alike: They're the same thing
Different: "Light-independent reaction" describes it functionally (doesn't directly need light). "Calvin cycle" is its name. Neither is more correct; they're two labels for one process.
Idea 8
Photosystem I & Photosystem II
Alike: Both are protein complexes in the thylakoid membrane that absorb light and pass excited electrons along the chain
Different: PSII (reaction center P680) comes first in the electron path and gets its replacement electrons from splitting water. PSI (reaction center P700) comes second and hands its excited electron to NADP⁺ to make NADPH.
Idea 7
Oxidation & reduction
Alike: Both describe a change in electrons during a redox reaction, and they always happen together
Different: Oxidation is losing electrons (water, becoming O₂). Reduction is gaining electrons (CO₂, becoming glucose).
Idea 6
P680 & P700
Alike: Both are reaction centers that get excited by a photon of light
Different: P680 is the reaction center of Photosystem II, excited first in the electron path. P700 is the reaction center of Photosystem I, excited second, and hands its electron toward NADPH.
Idea 7
Stoma & stroma
Alike: Both sound alike and both matter for photosynthesis
Different: A stoma is a pore in a leaf's surface that lets CO₂ in and O₂/water vapor out. The stroma is the fluid inside a chloroplast's inner membrane, where the Calvin cycle happens.
Idea 6, 8
Cyclic electron flow & non-cyclic electron flow
Alike: Both move electrons through the photosystems and make ATP
Different: Non-cyclic flow runs water → PSII → ETC → PSI → NADPH, making both ATP and NADPH. Cyclic flow reroutes the electron from PSI back into the ETC instead of to NADP⁺, making extra ATP but no new NADPH — used when the cell needs more ATP than NADPH.
Idea 7
Stroma & thylakoid space
Alike: Both are fluid-filled regions inside a chloroplast
Different: Stroma is the fluid outside the thylakoids but inside the inner membrane, where the Calvin cycle runs. Thylakoid space (lumen) is the compartment inside the thylakoid membrane itself, where H⁺ builds up during chemiosmosis.
Idea 6–7
Thylakoid & cristae
Alike: Both are folded internal membranes that increase surface area for energy reactions
Different: Thylakoids are flattened sacs in the chloroplast, stacked into grana, where the light reactions happen. Cristae are the folds of the mitochondrion's inner membrane (Unit 3b territory), where the electron transport chain of respiration happens.
Idea 6
RuBP & Rubisco
Alike: Both are essential to carbon fixation, the first step of the Calvin cycle
Different: RuBP is the 5-carbon sugar that CO₂ attaches to. Rubisco is the enzyme that attaches CO₂ to RuBP.
Idea 8
Intermembrane space & thylakoid space
Alike: Both are compartments created by a folded or double membrane where H⁺ can build up
Different: Intermembrane space is between the outer and inner membranes of the mitochondrion (Unit 3b). Thylakoid space is inside the thylakoid membrane of the chloroplast, where H⁺ accumulates during the light reactions.
Idea 7
PGA & PGAL (G3P)
Alike: Both are 3-carbon compounds in the Calvin cycle
Different: PGA (3-PGA) is made first, right when Rubisco fixes CO₂ onto RuBP. PGAL (G3P) is made next, after PGA is reduced using ATP and NADPH — it's the form that actually leaves the cycle or gets recycled into RuBP.
Idea 8
Cuticle & stoma
Alike: Both are leaf-surface features that manage water and gas exchange
Different: The cuticle is a waxy, waterproof coating over the whole leaf surface that prevents water loss. A stoma is a pore (that the cuticle does NOT cover) that opens and closes to let CO₂ in and water vapor/O₂ out.
Idea 6
Red light, green light, blue light & purple light
Alike: All are wavelengths of visible light that can hit a leaf
Different: Red and blue light are strongly absorbed by chlorophyll and drive photosynthesis. Green light is mostly reflected (not absorbed), which is why plants look green. Purple/violet light is also absorbed by accessory pigments, contributing to the action spectrum slightly differently than chlorophyll's absorption spectrum alone.
Idea 6
Electron transport chain (ETC) & photosystem
Alike: Both are groups of proteins embedded in a membrane that handle excited electrons
Different: A photosystem (PSI or PSII) absorbs light and excites an electron in the first place. The ETC is the chain of proteins that passes that already-excited electron along, releasing energy that pumps H⁺.
Idea 7
NADPH & NADH
Alike: Both are electron carriers that shuttle high-energy electrons/hydrogens to where they're needed
Different: NADPH is made in the light reactions of photosynthesis and spent in the Calvin cycle (this unit). NADH is made in cellular respiration (glycolysis, the Krebs cycle) and spent in the electron transport chain there — Unit 3b, not this guide.
Idea 7

LAB · CATALASE & HYDROGEN PEROXIDE

The question: what's catalase's actual temperature optimum?

Setup in one sentence: catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) into water and oxygen; measuring the O₂ produced at several different temperatures reveals the enzyme's temperature curve directly.

The experimental logic

  • Why temperature is the independent variable: it's the one thing deliberately changed between trials, with everything else (enzyme amount, substrate concentration, pH) held constant.
  • Why O₂ production is the dependent variable: it's what you measure, and it depends on how fast catalase is working — it's the thing you're trying to find out, not the thing you're controlling.
  • Why you'd want a negative control: a trial with no catalase added (or denatured catalase) shows how much O₂ shows up from H₂O₂ breaking down on its own, without the enzyme — so you know the enzyme, not something else, caused the O₂ you measured.
  • How to describe the trend using real numbers, not vague language: instead of "it went up then down," say something like "O₂ production rose from X% at 4°C to Y% at 34°C, then fell to Z% at 51°C" — cite the actual data points.

CASE STUDY · AMY1 & DIET

The question: does diet history predict AMY1 copy number?

What it's really asking: whether a population's long-term diet (starch-rich vs. protein-rich) correlates with how many copies of the AMY1 gene its members tend to carry — a real test of natural selection acting on an enzyme-related trait.

How to read the copy-number data

  • Compare average copy number between groups, not single individuals — natural selection shifts population averages, not everyone identically.
  • Connect copy number → amylase amount → starch-digesting ability → a real survival/reproductive advantage in a starch-heavy environment, step by step, rather than jumping straight to "more copies are better."
  • Watch for the trap of treating correlation as if every single farmer has more copies than every single hunter-gatherer — it's a population-level trend, with overlap between groups.

HER TEACHER'S GRAPHING RULES

A graph point is easy to earn and easy to lose.

  • Independent variable (with units) on the x-axis; dependent variable (with units) on the y-axis. The IV is what you change on purpose.
  • Continuous data gets a line graph, with the line actually drawn in connecting the points — not left as disconnected dots.
  • Title the graph "The effect of [IV] on [DV]."
  • Label both axes with a title AND units.
  • Calculate your error bars before you scale the axes, so the scale you pick actually fits them.
  • Never extrapolate past your actual data.
  • No color — tests are scanned in grayscale.

How the Unit 3a Test is graded, and how to get an A

The test is half multiple choice, half FRQ, with the AP formula sheet and a calculator, no retests. That's why every day in the Study Lab has a written answer, not just multiple choice.

Her teacher's FRQ-writing rules

  • Write in full sentences, never bullet points, outlines, or diagrams alone. A diagram without full labels earns nothing.
  • Never use "it" or "stuff." Always name the actual subject: "the enzyme," "the H⁺ gradient," not "it."
  • When asked to compare two things, address BOTH explicitly, not just one.
  • Always include units when identifying a variable.
  • For "Identify / Predict / Determine" verbs, one clear sentence is enough. Don't over-explain — that wastes time without earning extra points.
  • For "Explain," cite the actual mechanism — which bonds, which structures, which process — never just restate the vocabulary word.
  • Don't contradict an earlier correct statement, even accidentally. A contradiction can cost a point you already earned.
  • Always finish any calculation to a decimal, never leave it as a fraction.
  • For graphs: draw the line in, label both axes with title and units, and never extrapolate past the real data.

Writing Unit 3a FRQs

Unit 3a FRQs usually ask you to explain a mechanism — not just name a vocabulary word. A few sentence starters to build from:

"The rate decreased because the [specific bonds] broke, disrupting the [specific structure], which [caused this downstream effect]."

"[Substance] moves from [PSII/PSI/the thylakoid space/the stroma] to [the next location] by [process], carrying [what it's carrying]."

"The [curve] plateaus/rises in a straight line because [the actual reason — saturation, or substrate never being limiting]."
  • Name the actual structure or bond. "The active site's H-bonds," not "its shape."
  • Describe = what it is. Explain = how or why. "Describe the Calvin cycle" wants the three phases in order. "Explain why it takes two turns to make glucose" wants the 1 G3P per turn vs. 2 G3P per glucose reasoning.
  • For "predict" questions about inhibitors or temperature/pH shifts, walk from the mechanism to the rate, not the other way around.
  • For Calvin cycle totals, show the doubling logic (why ×2) rather than just stating 6 CO₂/12 NADPH/18 ATP.
  • Connect to Units 1–2 when it fits. "The same H-bonds that hold the enzyme's 3° structure" shows understanding and often earns the explanation point.

Why isn't "breaking the phosphate bond releases energy" correct?
Breaking any bond costs energy. ATP hydrolysis is exergonic because the products (ADP + phosphate) are more stable/lower-energy than ATP — partly because separating ATP's three crowded, negatively charged phosphates releases their mutual repulsion, like a coiled spring.
What does an enzyme change about a reaction, and what does it NOT change?
It lowers the activation energy (Eₐ), speeding the reaction up. It does NOT change the starting or ending energy levels (the overall free energy change) of the reaction.
What two things must match for a substrate to fit an active site?
Both shape AND charge — not shape alone.
Why is the temperature-vs-rate curve asymmetric but the pH-vs-rate curve symmetric?
Heat builds up and suddenly breaks the bonds holding 3°/4° structure (denaturation) — a steep, permanent crash. pH shifts gradually disrupt ionic interactions at a similar rate in either direction, giving a smoother, symmetric curve.
Rate vs. [substrate] plateaus. Rate vs. [enzyme] doesn't. Why the difference?
Substrate concentration plateaus once every active site is occupied (saturation) — more substrate can't speed things up further. Enzyme concentration doesn't plateau because substrate is in excess, so it's never the limiting factor.
Competitive or noncompetitive: which binds the active site itself?
Competitive. Noncompetitive (allosteric) binds elsewhere and changes the enzyme's shape instead.
What does feedback inhibition usually shut off, and why?
Usually the first enzyme in a multi-step pathway — so the cell stops making more of something it already has enough of.
Why does the real photosynthesis equation show 12 H₂O in but only 6 H₂O out?
The 12 that go in are split apart in the light reactions (photolysis). The 6 that come out are made fresh during the Calvin cycle — they aren't the same water molecules passing through.
In photosynthesis, what gets oxidized and what gets reduced?
Water is oxidized (loses electrons, becomes O₂). CO₂ is reduced (gains electrons, becomes glucose).
Put in order: P700, water splitting, ATP synthase, P680, NADPH formation.
Water splitting (replaces PSII's electrons) → P680 (PSII) → ATP synthase (via the H⁺ gradient the ETC built) → P700 (PSI) → NADPH formation.
Why does cyclic electron flow exist?
The Calvin cycle needs more ATP than NADPH (not a 1:1 ratio). Cyclic flow reroutes the electron from PSI back into the ETC to make extra ATP without making more NADPH, letting the cell balance the ratio.
Name the three phases of the Calvin cycle, in order.
Carbon fixation (Rubisco attaches CO₂ to RuBP, making PGA) → Reduction (PGA becomes G3P using ATP and NADPH) → Regeneration (the leftover G3P is rebuilt into RuBP using more ATP).
Why does the Calvin cycle have to turn twice to make one glucose?
Only 1 G3P leaves the cycle per turn, but it takes 2 G3P to build 1 glucose — so two turns are needed, using 6 CO₂, 12 NADPH, and 18 ATP in total.
A catalase trial shows rising O₂ production from 4°C to 34°C, then a crash at 51°C. What does that tell you?
The enzyme's real-world optimum is somewhere in the low-to-mid 30s°C; by 51°C it has already started denaturing, matching the asymmetric temperature curve.
What does the AMY1 story show about natural selection?
Populations with long histories of starch-rich diets tend to carry more AMY1 gene copies on average than populations with low-starch diets — natural selection acting on an enzyme-related gene within human history.
