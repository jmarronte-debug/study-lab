"Life depends on molecules interacting. Energy transfer depends on molecular interactions." And: you do not need to draw Lewis dot structures for the AP exam.

IDEA 1

Like attracts like. Polarity decides who hangs out with whom.

In plain English: Some molecules have lopsided charge (polar) and some don't (nonpolar). Polar sticks to polar, nonpolar sticks to nonpolar, and they avoid each other — that's why oil separates from water. Almost everything about water, membranes, and protein folding follows from this one sentence.

Why water is polar. Oxygen is highly electronegative — it pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so the O end is partly negative (δ−) and the H ends are partly positive (δ+). That lets each water molecule form hydrogen bonds with up to four others. Covalent bonds are within a molecule; hydrogen bonds are the (weak, individually) attraction between molecules. H-bonds are the strongest kind of intermolecular force but still far weaker than covalent bonds.

"Presence of O and N are quick clues of polar molecules or polar areas of molecules." Hydrocarbons (just C and H) are nonpolar.

Just say NO to polarity — N and O make things polar. Get your polarity ON.

Trap she used in class: the water-vs-ethanol graph. Water heats more slowly because its hydrogen bonds absorb energy before the temperature rises — not because ethanol has stronger bonds. Answer: water's H-bonds absorb more energy before temperature increases.

pH in one breath

pH measures H⁺ concentration. Acids donate H⁺ → low pH. Bases take up H⁺ → high pH. Living things tolerate only a narrow range and use buffers to resist pH change. "You will NOT need to calculate pH in this class."

Bond strength ladder (she'll ask)

Covalent (electrons shared; strongest; within molecules; broken by enzymes, not by heat at life temps)  ›  Ionic (electrons transferred; salts like NaCl; she calls them weak because they ionize — fall apart — in water)  ›  Hydrogen bond (weak individually, powerful in numbers; between molecules). Polar covalent = shared unequally; nonpolar covalent = shared equally. Double bonds are stronger, shorter, and "kinky" compared to single.

IDEA 2

Seven functional groups tell you how a molecule behaves.

In plain English: A functional group is a small cluster of atoms bolted onto a carbon skeleton. Spot the group and you instantly know whether that part of the molecule is polar, charged, acidic or basic — and therefore what it'll do. Your teacher said to practice these until you recognize them quickly. This is the single highest-yield table on the page.

Grading trap from her slides: when circling groups, circle only the atoms in the group. On a nucleotide, circle the phosphate; don't scoop up the sugar's –OH groups with it.

Skeletal drawings (she tested this)

In a zig-zag line drawing, every corner is a carbon, and any bonds not drawn are filled with hydrogens. Count corners to count carbons; then fill each carbon to 4 bonds to count H. Carbon is the backbone of all macromolecules because it makes 4 stable covalent bonds — chains, branches, rings, single/double/triple bonds — and forms isomers (same formula, different shape, different function; thalidomide's two mirror forms: one a sedative, one caused birth defects).

IDEA 3

Build by removing water. Break by adding water.

In plain English: Big molecules (polymers) are chains of small repeating units (monomers). To link two monomers, the cell pulls an –H off one and an –OH off the other — that's a water molecule leaving — and a covalent bond forms where they were. To break the chain, add water back. That's it. Two reactions explain how every macromolecule is made and digested.

The formula trick she asked for: glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ + fructose C₆H₁₂O₆ − H₂O = C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (sucrose). Two monomers, subtract one water. A triglyceride releases 3 waters (three ester bonds).

*Her slide flags lipids with a red asterisk: "it is a little more complicated than this." If asked, say lipids don't have a true monomer/polymer structure.

Carbohydrate details she emphasized

  • -ose means sugar. Triose C₃H₆O₃, pentose C₅H₁₀O₅ (ribose, deoxyribose), hexose C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose, fructose, galactose). Names ending in -ose are always carbs, but not all carbs end in -ose (starch).
  • Disaccharides: sucrose = glucose + fructose (table sugar); lactose = glucose + galactose (milk); maltose = glucose + glucose.
  • Polysaccharides are all chains of glucose. Storage: starch (plants), glycogen (animals — liver and muscle). Structure: cellulose (plant cell walls), chitin (insect shells, fungal cell walls).
  • Structure → function #1: starch has alpha linkages, cellulose has beta linkages. We have enzymes for alpha, not beta, so cellulose is indigestible fiber. Cows manage it only because bacteria in their gut do it for them.
  • Structure → function #2: glycogen is more branched than starch → more free ends → enzymes can hydrolyze it faster → quicker energy release.
  • Number sugar carbons starting to the right of the ring oxygen. Deoxyribose is missing an O on the 2′ carbon.

Lipid details she emphasized

  • Triglyceride = glycerol (3 C, each with –OH) + 3 fatty acids (long hydrocarbon chain ending in –COOH). Three ester bonds, three waters released.
  • Saturated = all single C–C bonds, straight, packs tightly, solid at room temp, animal fats (butter). Unsaturated = one or more double bonds, kinked, liquid, plant oils. Diversity of fats comes from varying chain length, saturation, and which fatty acids sit on the glycerol.
  • Phospholipid = glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate group. Amphipathic: polar hydrophilic head, nonpolar hydrophobic tails. That's why they self-assemble into a bilayer (heads out toward water, tails in) — the basis of every cell membrane.
  • Steroids = four fused carbon rings + functional groups. Changing the functional groups changes the function: cholesterol (membrane fluidity, precursor to the others), cortisol (stress), estrogen and testosterone (sex hormones).
IDEA 5

A protein's shape is its job — and four levels of bonds hold the shape.

In plain English: A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a specific 3-D shape, and the shape is what lets it do its job. The chain's order is locked in by strong covalent bonds; the folding is held by weaker interactions. So heat or acid can unfold a protein (and kill its function) without breaking the chain itself.

Amino acid anatomy: a central carbon with an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen, and an R-group. All 20 share the same backbone; only the R-group differs, and the R-group's chemistry (nonpolar / polar uncharged / polar charged) drives folding. Peptide bonds form between one amino acid's carboxyl and the next one's amino group, so chains have one-way directionality: amino (N) terminus → carboxyl (C) terminus.

LevelWhat it isHeld byInvolves
Primary 1°The sequence (order) of amino acids — determined by DNACovalent peptide bondsBackbone
Secondary 2°Local folding into α-helix (spiral) or β-pleated sheet (zig-zag)Hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H of the backboneBackbone only — R-groups NOT involved
Tertiary 3°Overall 3-D shape of one polypeptideH-bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, disulfide bridges (the only covalent one, so the strongest)R-groups
Quaternary 4°Two or more polypeptides assembled into one protein (hemoglobin = 4 subunits). Not all proteins have itSame interactions as 3°R-groups of different chains

Which R-groups do what: polar R-groups → hydrogen bonds. Charged (acidic/basic) R-groups → ionic bonds. Nonpolar R-groups → hydrophobic interactions (cluster inside, away from water). Cysteine's –SH → disulfide bridges.

Denaturing — the FRQ favorite

Heat, pH change, or salinity break the weak interactions → 2°, 3°, 4° unfold → structural change → functional change. The 1° sequence survives because peptide bonds are covalent and need an enzyme (hydrolysis) to break. Her examples: cooking an egg (heat denatures albumin), vinegar on a raw egg (pH denatures it — looks cooked), hair straightening (chemicals break and re-form disulfide bonds, changing 3°/4°; it grows out because new hair is made and the chemicals wear off), an enzyme in the wrong pH losing its shape and therefore its function.

Trap: a mutation swapping a polar R-group for a nonpolar one changes 1° for certain, may or may not change 2° (depends on location), and most likely changes 3°/4° because R-group interactions stabilize those levels.
IDEA 6

Nucleic acids store information in the order of four bases.

In plain English: DNA and RNA are chains of nucleotides. The sugar-phosphate parts form a repeating backbone; the bases stick out and their sequence is the message. Bases on opposite strands pair by hydrogen bonds — weak enough that the cell can unzip the strands to read them.

A nucleotide has three parts: a phosphate group, a 5-carbon sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and a nitrogenous base. Nucleotides link by phosphodiester bonds (covalent) into a strand with a 5′ phosphate end and a 3′ hydroxyl end; new nucleotides are added to the 3′ end. DNA is an antiparallel double helix — the two strands run in opposite 5′→3′ directions.

The FRQ recipe

Your teacher's slides are unusually explicit about how FRQs are scored. Follow the recipe exactly and you collect points that most of the class will leave behind.

Step 1 — Read the prompt three times

  1. First, look at any data, table or model. Ask: what am I seeing and what does it mean?
  2. Then read the prompt for the bold task verbs. What exactly am I being asked to do?
  3. Last, read it again and ask: which biology concept is this really about? (Usually one of the six ideas.)

"MOST IMPORTANT: READ THE PROMPT!" and "ANSWER the question being asked, not the one you thought should have been asked."

Step 2 — Match your answer's length to the verb

IdentifySHORT

Name it. One phrase. No explanation needed — extra words earn nothing and cost time.

PredictSHORT

State the expected cause or effect. Quick.

DescribeSHORTER

Give the relevant characteristics. Do NOT explain how or why.

ExplainLONGER

Say how (the process) or why (the reason) using evidence or reasoning. This is where points live.

JustifyLONGER

Point to specific evidence (data) that supports a claim, then explain why/how that evidence supports it.

ConstructGRAPH

Graph the data. Scored on graph type, labels, and data + error bars. Pencil for the graph, pen for everything else.

The 2-minute drill. If you're running out of time, skip to every remaining Identify and Predict part first. They're 1 point each and take seconds.

Step 3 — Write like the rubric is reading

  • Be specific. Never "it" — name the molecule, the group, the population.
  • Complete sentences in paragraph form. No bullets.
  • Don't restate the prompt. Zero credit, wasted time.
  • Units with every number. No unit, no credit.
  • Direction of trends. Increasing, decreasing, or constant — say which.
  • Compare BOTH sides. If asked to compare two groups, address both explicitly.
  • Cause before effect. "Because heat breaks H-bonds, the protein unfolds and loses function" — not the other way round.
  • Connect concepts. Questions are built to make you link two ideas (e.g., functional groups → polarity → protein folding).
  • Answer every part. A blank is a guaranteed zero; an attempt might score.

Step 4 — If there's a graph

  • Bar graph for discontinuous data (categories, e.g. three plant populations); line graph for continuous data (time, temperature).
  • X-axis = independent variable, Y-axis = dependent variable, both labeled with units copied exactly from the data table.
  • Scale intervals simple and even; anchor the scale at a number (usually 0).
  • Plot accurately; draw error bars (95% CI = mean ± 2 SEM — calculate them on scratch paper first so they fit on the grid) and don't shade over the lower bars.
  • No points for a title. Don't rely on color — FRQs are scanned in black and white.

Timing for a 40-minute section

Spend the first minute scanning every question and marking the short verbs. Then work in order, but leave any part you're stuck on and come back. Save the last 3 minutes to scan for blanks and to add units you forgot. A blank part is the only unfixable mistake.

What she's likely to ask

MCQ-style patterns to expect

  • Given a structure drawing: which macromolecule is it, which functional groups are present, is this region polar or nonpolar? (Her Structure ID homework, exactly.)
  • Count carbons and hydrogens in a skeletal (zig-zag) drawing.
  • Write or pick the formula: a hexose, a pentose, a disaccharide (subtract water!).
  • Name the specific bond: glycosidic / ester / peptide / phosphodiester — and know they're all covalent.
  • Which property of water explains this observation? (strider, sweating, coastal climate, xylem, floating ice)
  • Which level of protein structure is affected by… (heat / a mutation / breaking disulfide bonds)?
  • Saturated vs unsaturated: which is solid at room temperature and why?
  • Purine vs pyrimidine; DNA vs RNA differences; complementary base sequence.
  • Data interpretation with a small graph, like the water-vs-ethanol heating curve.

FRQ prompts to practice (with what a full answer needs)

Explain how the structure of a phospholipid enables it to form cell membranes.
Needs: phospholipid = glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate → amphipathic: polar/hydrophilic phosphate head, nonpolar/hydrophobic fatty-acid tails. Because like attracts like, in water the heads face outward toward water and tails face inward away from it, so phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer — a stable barrier between the watery inside and outside of the cell.
Describe the difference between starch and cellulose, then explain why humans can digest one but not the other.
Describe (short): both are polysaccharides of glucose; starch has alpha glycosidic linkages, cellulose has beta linkages, so they have different shapes. Explain (longer): humans have enzymes shaped to fit and hydrolyze alpha linkages but lack enzymes for beta linkages, so cellulose passes through as fiber. Herbivores manage because gut bacteria produce the needed enzyme.
An enzyme is placed in a solution with a much lower pH than its normal environment. Predict the effect on the enzyme and justify your prediction.
Predict (short): the enzyme will denature and lose its function. Justify: the low pH (high H⁺) disrupts the hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds between R-groups that hold the 2°, 3° (and 4°) structure; the protein unfolds → structural change → functional change. The 1° structure is unaffected because peptide bonds are covalent.
A mutation replaces a polar amino acid with a nonpolar one. Explain how each level of protein structure might be affected.
1° — definitely changed (new sequence). 2° — may or may not change; depends on where it sits, since 2° is backbone H-bonding. 3° and 4° — most likely changed, because R-group interactions stabilize them: a nonpolar R-group can't form the H-bond the polar one did and will be pulled toward the hydrophobic interior, altering the fold and therefore the function.
Explain why water has a higher specific heat than ethanol, using the heating-curve data.
Water's temperature rose more slowly per unit energy (state the direction of the trend, with units from the graph). Water forms more/stronger hydrogen bonds between molecules; the added energy is first used to break H-bonds before it can increase molecular motion (temperature). So more energy is needed per degree — high specific heat.
Identify the type of bond formed between two amino acids, and describe the reaction that forms it.
Identify: a peptide bond (covalent). Describe: dehydration synthesis — the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next lose an –OH and an –H, releasing a water molecule, and a C–N bond forms.
Saturated vs unsaturated fats: describe the structural difference and explain why one is solid at room temperature.
Describe: saturated fatty acids have only single C–C bonds and are straight; unsaturated have one or more double bonds that put kinks in the chain. Explain: straight saturated chains pack tightly together, allowing more intermolecular attraction, so they're solid at room temperature (butter, animal fat). Kinked unsaturated chains can't pack tightly, so they stay liquid (plant oils).

Rapid self-test

Twenty-three quick-recall checks for any spare five minutes — a bus ride, a lunch line. Say the answer out loud before tapping; if you hesitated, it goes on the misses list. For scored practice with explanations, use the Practice Lab.

Which particle identifies the element? Which changes in an ion? Which changes between isotopes?
Protons identify the element. An ion has gained or lost electrons. Isotopes differ in neutrons.
Which four elements make up about 96% of living matter, and which four cover most of the rest?
C, H, O, N; then P, S, Ca, K.
What is electronegativity?
An atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a bond. Oxygen's high electronegativity is why water is polar.
Which two elements are the quick clue that a molecule (or region) is polar?
N and O. Just say NO to polarity.
Hydrogen bonds form within or between water molecules?
Between. Polar covalent bonds are within. One water molecule = 3 atoms and up to 4 H-bonds.
General formula for carbohydrates? Formula for a hexose?
(CH₂O)ₙ; hexose = C₆H₁₂O₆.
Sucrose is made of which two monosaccharides, and what's its formula?
Glucose + fructose; C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆ − H₂O = C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.
Name the bond between: two sugars / glycerol and a fatty acid / two amino acids / two nucleotides.
Glycosidic / ester / peptide / phosphodiester. All covalent, all formed by dehydration synthesis.
Which functional group is polar, negatively charged, AND acidic — name both.
Carboxyl (–COOH) and phosphate (–PO₄). Amino is polar, positively charged, basic.
Aldehyde vs ketone?
Both are carbonyls (C=O). Aldehyde: at the end of the chain. Ketone: kaptured in the middle.
Storage polysaccharide in plants? In animals? Structural in plants? In animals/fungi?
Starch / glycogen / cellulose / chitin.
Why is glycogen digested faster than starch?
It's more branched → more free ends → enzymes hydrolyze at more sites at once.
Fat vs phospholipid — what's the same, what's different?
Both have glycerol + fatty acids joined by ester bonds. Fat has 3 fatty acids; phospholipid has 2 fatty acids + a phosphate group, making it amphipathic.
What makes a steroid a steroid? Name the precursor steroid.
Four fused carbon rings + functional groups. Cholesterol is the precursor (also keeps membranes fluid).
Which protein level uses hydrogen bonds between the backbone only?
Secondary (2°) — α-helix and β-pleated sheet. R-groups NOT involved.
Which bond in tertiary structure is covalent, and which amino acid makes it?
Disulfide bridge, from the –SH (sulfhydryl) groups of two cysteines.
Heat denatures a protein. Which level is NOT affected and why?
Primary — peptide bonds are covalent and require an enzyme (hydrolysis) to break; heat only disrupts the weaker interactions holding 2°, 3°, 4°.
Three parts of a nucleotide?
Phosphate group, 5-carbon sugar, nitrogenous base.
Purines vs pyrimidines — which are which, and how many H-bonds in A–T vs C–G?
Purines (2 rings): A, G. Pyrimidines (1 ring): C, U, T. A–T: 2 H-bonds. C–G: 3.
Three structural differences between DNA and RNA?
Deoxyribose vs ribose; thymine vs uracil; double-stranded vs usually single-stranded.
Which direction are new nucleotides added to a growing strand?
To the 3′ end. Strands run 5′→3′ and the two DNA strands are antiparallel.
Which two water properties together move water up a plant's xylem?
Cohesion (water to water) and adhesion (water to xylem walls) → capillary action.
A FRQ says "Describe." Do you explain why?
No. Describe = characteristics only, shorter answer. Save the how/why for Explain and Justify.
