The big picture

One sentence for the whole unit: a cell is a bag of Unit 1 molecules, divided into compartments, with a membrane that decides what gets in and out.

Every topic in Unit 2 answers one of four questions:

  1. What's inside? The organelles, and what each one does (2.1–2.2).
  2. How big can it be? Surface area to volume (2.2).
  3. What's the wall made of, and what gets through? The membrane (2.3–2.4) and how things cross it: diffusion, osmosis, pumps, and water potential (2.5–2.8).
  4. Why compartments, and where did they come from? Compartmentalization and endosymbiosis (2.9–2.10).

IDEA 1 · 2.1

Every living thing is made of cells, and cells come in two basic designs.

In plain English: A cell is the smallest thing that can do everything life does. Some cells are simple, with everything floating together in one open room (prokaryotes: bacteria). Others are divided into rooms with walls, each room with its own job (eukaryotes: plants, animals, fungi). Those "rooms" are organelles, and their walls are membranes.

Cell theory, three parts

All living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the basic unit of life. Cells only come from other cells. The last one is why the teacher says "life is continuous": every cell in you traces back, unbroken, to earlier cells.

Prokaryote vs. eukaryote

Tripping point: "Prokaryotes have no organelles" is a trap answer. They have ribosomes, which count as organelles in many textbooks. What they lack is membrane-bound organelles. When in doubt, say "membrane-bound." Every cell, even the simplest, has a plasma membrane, ribosomes, DNA, and cytoplasm, because every cell has to make proteins.

Plant vs. animal cells

Plants have three things animals don't: a cell wall (cellulose, the β-glucose polymer from Unit 1), chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole. Both have mitochondria. Plants need mitochondria too, because photosynthesis makes sugar but the plant still has to turn that sugar into ATP.

Tripping point: "Plants have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria" is wrong. Plants have both.

Microscopes (one quick fact each)

Light microscope: uses light, sees living cells, limited detail. TEM (transmission electron): electrons pass through a thin slice, so it shows the inside. SEM (scanning electron): electrons bounce off the surface, so it gives a 3-D look at the outside. Memory hook: TEM = Through, SEM = Surface.

IDEA 2 · 2.1–2.2

Follow one protein and you've learned half the organelles.

In plain English: Instead of memorizing a list of organelles, follow a protein from the moment it's made until it leaves the cell. The organelles it passes through in order are the endomembrane system. Once you know the route, each organelle's job is obvious from where it sits on the route.

The route of a secreted protein, like a digestive enzyme

  1. Nucleus: holds the DNA instructions for the protein. Unit 1: nucleic acids store information.
  2. Ribosome on the rough ER: reads the instructions and links amino acids with peptide bonds. Unit 1: dehydration synthesis.
  3. Rough ER: the new protein goes inside, starts folding, and gets its first sugar tags. It's "rough" because it's studded with ribosomes.
  4. Transport vesicle: a small bubble of membrane buds off and carries the protein to the Golgi.
  5. Golgi apparatus: modifies (adds or trims carbohydrates to make glycoproteins), packages and sorts proteins by destination.
  6. Secretory vesicle → plasma membrane: the vesicle fuses with the membrane and releases the protein outside (exocytosis).
ORDER
Nucleus → Ribosome/Rough ER → Vesicle → Golgi → Vesicle → Membrane. The key point: ER before Golgi. The most common mistake is swapping them.

Free ribosomes vs. bound ribosomes (her review sheet asks this)

Free ribosomesBound ribosomes
WhereFloating in the cytosolAttached to the rough ER

The ribosomes themselves are identical; only the destination of the protein differs. So: "Where is an enzyme that's used in the cytosol made?" On free ribosomes. "Where is a protein hormone that goes into the blood made?" On ribosomes bound to the rough ER, then it follows the route above and leaves by exocytosis.

IDEA 3 · 2.1

The cytoskeleton is the cell's frame, and its tracks.

In plain English: Inside the cell is a network of protein fibers. Some give it shape, some hold things in place, and some are highways that motor proteins "walk" along, carrying cargo.
FiberMade ofShapeJob
Microtubules ⭐Tubulin (globular protein)Hollow tubes, the thickestTracks for vesicles; move chromosomes during cell division; form cilia and flagella
MicrofilamentsActinSolid thin rodsChange cell shape; muscle contraction
Intermediate filamentsFibrous proteinsRope-likeResist pulling (tension); anchor organelles in place

Cilia and flagella are both built from microtubules in a 9 + 2 pattern (nine pairs in a ring, two in the middle). Cilia are short and many; flagella are long and few.

Motor proteins: dynein bends cilia and flagella by sliding the microtubule pairs; kinesin carries vesicles along microtubules. Unit 1 link: both work by changing shape (a "conformational change"). That's the tertiary structure idea again: a protein's shape is its function, so changing its shape makes it move.

Tripping point: tight junctions seal; gap junctions connect. If a question mentions "leak-proof," it's tight. If it mentions "communication" or "passing molecules," it's gap junctions (or plasmodesmata in plants).

IDEA 4 · 2.2

Why cells stay small: surface area vs. volume.

In plain English: Everything a cell needs comes in through its surface, and all its waste goes out the same way. The surface is the "doors"; the volume is the "people inside." As a cell grows, the number of people grows much faster than the number of doors. Eventually there aren't enough doors to keep everyone fed. So cells stay small, or fold their surface, or divide.

Watch the numbers (cubes, the way the lab did it)

SideSurface area (6s²)Volume (s³)SA : V
1 cm6 cm²1 cm³6:1
2 cm24 cm²8 cm³3:1
4 cm96 cm²64 cm³1.5:1

Double the size → the SA:V ratio is cut in half. For a cube, SA:V is just 6 ÷ side length, so bigger always means a smaller ratio.

HER FORMAT
Her review sheet says: always express SA:V as SA:1. Write "3:1", not "3" or "24:8". In the Study Lab, type it that way too (for example 3:1).
Tripping points:
• "As a cell grows, its surface area decreases." Wrong. The surface area goes up; the ratio goes down. Read carefully for the word "ratio."
• Don't memorize the formulas. They're on the AP formula sheet. Know how to use them, and always include units (cm², cm³).
• A higher SA:V is better for exchange. Small cells win.

The same idea, everywhere in biology (big-picture thread)

Whenever living things need more exchange, they increase surface area: the villi and microvilli of the intestine (absorbing food), the folded inner membrane of mitochondria (more room for reactions), the folded ER, and later root hairs and lungs. You've already seen this in Unit 1: glycogen is highly branched, and more branches means more ends where enzymes can work at once. Same principle.

Why not infinitely small? A cell still has to fit its DNA, ribosomes, and everything needed to carry out life. That sets the lower limit.

IDEA 5 · 2.3

The membrane is Unit 1 chemistry, assembled.

In plain English: A membrane is two layers of phospholipids with proteins floating in it. Water is on both sides of a cell (inside and out), so the water-loving heads face out on both sides and the water-fearing tails hide in the middle. Nobody has to build it: phospholipids arrange themselves this way automatically because of like attracts like.

"Fluid mosaic model," decoded

Fluid: the phospholipids and most proteins drift sideways; the membrane is more like olive oil than plastic wrap. Mosaic: it's made of many different pieces (phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, carbohydrate chains) fitted together.

PartWhat it doesUnit 1 link
PhospholipidsForm the bilayer, the basic barrierAmphipathic: polar head, nonpolar tails
CholesterolKeeps fluidity steady as temperature changesA steroid (four fused rings)
Integral proteinsGo into or all the way through the membrane: channels, transporters, receptorsR-groups decide where they sit
Peripheral proteinsSit on the surface—
Glycoproteins / glycolipidsCarbohydrate chains on the outside that act as "ID tags" for cell recognitionCarbohydrates + glycosidic bonds

Cholesterol: the "fluidity buffer"

This trips up nearly everyone, because cholesterol does opposite things at different temperatures. Think of it as a buffer, the same idea as pH buffers in Unit 1: it keeps things from swinging too far either way.

TemperatureProblem without cholesterolWhat cholesterol does
WarmMembrane gets too runnyDecreases fluidity (holds the tails back)
ColdMembrane gets too stiff (tails pack tightly)Increases fluidity (acts as a spacer between tails)

Unit 1 link: the cold problem is the saturated-fat problem. Straight tails pack tightly and go solid, like butter. Cholesterol wedges in between them, the same way the kinks of unsaturated fats keep oil liquid.

Membrane proteins: which parts face where

A protein that crosses the membrane has a middle section inside the oily tail layer and ends sticking out into the water on each side. So its middle has nonpolar (hydrophobic) R-groups and its ends have polar (hydrophilic) R-groups. This is the slide 11 "label the protein" question, and it's pure Unit 1.

The phospholipid "orientation" question (slide 9)

SetupWhat forms
Water above, water belowA bilayer: tails face each other in the middle
Oil above, oil belowA reversed bilayer: heads in the middle, tails out toward the oil
Water above, oil belowA single layer: heads up into the water, tails down into the oil

You don't need to memorize these. In every case, heads go toward water and tails go toward oil, and the answer follows.

IDEA 6 · 2.4

What gets through: size and charge decide.

In plain English: The middle of the membrane is oily. Anything that can dissolve in oil (small, nonpolar things) slips right through. Anything charged or large and polar gets stuck and needs a protein doorway. That's what "selectively permeable" means.
Kind of moleculeExamplesCrosses the bilayer by itself?
Small, nonpolarO₂, CO₂, N₂Yes, easily
Large, nonpolarSteroid hormonesYes, slowly
Small, polar, unchargedWater, ureaA little; most water goes through aquaporins (water channels)
Large, polarGlucoseNo: needs a protein
Ions (any size)Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺No: needs a protein
RULE
Charge beats size. A sodium ion is tiny, but it's charged, so it can't cross the oily middle. A steroid is big, but it's nonpolar, so it can. Ask "Is it charged?" first, then "Is it polar?", then "Is it small?"
Tripping points:
• CO₂ is nonpolar, even though its bonds are polar. The two C=O bonds pull equally in opposite directions and cancel out. It crosses easily.
• Water is polar but still crosses, because it's so small. Most of it uses aquaporins, though.
• In the POGIL, urea crossed more easily than glucose. Both are polar; urea is smaller. Size matters once charge is ruled out.

The cell wall

Plants, fungi, and bacteria have a wall outside the plasma membrane. All cell walls are made of complex carbohydrates, and all give structure and help regulate what enters. They differ in which carbohydrate: cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, peptidoglycan in bacteria. Unit 1 link: cellulose is the β-glucose polymer humans can't digest, and chitin is the same material as insect exoskeletons.

Tripping point: the cell wall does not replace the membrane. Plant cells have both; the wall is outside it.

IDEA 7 · 2.5

Downhill is free. Uphill costs energy.

In plain English: Molecules are always jiggling around at random. Because of that, they spread out from where they're crowded to where they're not. That's diffusion, and it happens on its own, with no energy. Moving something the other way, into a place where it's already crowded, is like pushing a ball uphill: the cell has to spend energy (usually ATP) and use a pump.

This is her Blue Sheet table. The big-picture link: Idea 6 decides Idea 7. If a molecule can cross the oily middle on its own (small, nonpolar), it uses simple diffusion. If it can't (charged, or large and polar), it needs a protein.

Moving big things: bulk transport (uses energy)

NAMES
Endo = in, exo = out, phago = eat, pino = drink, receptor-mediated = only what fits the receptor. On her slide, the teacher asks you to explain receptor-mediated endocytosis from its name. That's the skill.
Tripping points:
• Never write "it moves from high to low." Her rule: name the substance and say concentration. "Glucose moves from an area of high glucose concentration to an area of low glucose concentration."
• "With the gradient" = downhill = passive. "Against the gradient" = uphill = active. Students swap these under pressure.
• A channel doesn't make transport active. Facilitated diffusion uses a protein and is still passive. Only pumps use energy.
• Diffusion doesn't stop at equilibrium. Molecules keep moving both ways; only the net movement stops (dynamic equilibrium).

IDEA 8 · 2.6–2.7

Water follows solute.

In plain English: Water moves toward wherever there's more stuff dissolved. That's the whole idea of osmosis. Why? Dissolved particles hold water molecules around themselves (hydration shells), so a salty or sugary side has less free water. Free water diffuses from where there's more of it to where there's less, which is the same as moving toward more solute.
SAME IDEA
All three sentences mean the same thing, and the teacher uses all three:
• Water moves from high [H₂O] to low [H₂O].
• Water moves from hypotonic to hypertonic (low solute → high solute).
• Water moves from high water potential to low water potential (Idea 9).

Osmolarity (a word on her slides and review sheet) is simply the total concentration of dissolved particles in a solution. High osmolarity = lots of solute = hypertonic. So "water moves from low osmolarity to high osmolarity" is one more way to say "hypotonic to hypertonic." Note that NaCl counts double, because each unit becomes two particles (the same reason i = 2 in Idea 9).

Tonicity words are always comparisons

WordMeansWater moves…
HypertonicMORE solute (than the other side)toward it
HypotonicLESS solute (than the other side)away from it
IsotonicSAME soluteboth ways equally: no net movement (dynamic equilibrium)

Say the comparison every time: "The beaker is hypotonic compared to the cell." Her test notes take points off for incomplete comparisons.

What happens to cells (her Tonicity slide)

Cell is in a…Animal cell (no wall)Plant cell (has a wall)
Hypotonic solution (water rushes in)Lysed: swells and may burstTurgid: firm. This is the healthy state for plants.
Isotonic solutionNormal: the healthy state for animal cellsFlaccid: limp; the plant wilts

Idea 6 link: plant cells survive a hypotonic world because of the cell wall. Idea 1 link: the central vacuole fills with water and pushes out (turgor pressure).

Facilitated diffusion: passive, with a protein doorway

Charged and large polar molecules can't cross the oily middle (Idea 6), so they go through channel proteins (a tunnel) or carrier proteins (change shape to pass the molecule through). It's still downhill, "with" the gradient, so it costs no energy. Aquaporins are channels just for water, which is why water crosses membranes so quickly.

Osmoregulation: why this matters for survival

Organisms have to keep their water and solute balance (homeostasis). A freshwater fish lives in water that's hypotonic to its body, so water keeps flowing in and it must get rid of it. A saltwater fish has the opposite problem. Plant roots are kept hypertonic to the soil (lots of dissolved sugars), so water flows from the soil into the roots. For Monday's case study "Osmosis is Serious Business," use the same method on every scenario: first decide what's hypertonic compared to what, then say which way water moves and what happens to the cells.

Tripping points:
• Only the solute that CAN'T cross decides where water goes. In her "Do you understand osmosis?" slide, glucose and fructose can cross, so they just even out. Sucrose is trapped (0.03 M inside vs. 0.01 M outside), so the cell is hypertonic and water moves in.
• "Hyper" describes the solution, not the water. A hypertonic solution has less free water.
• Water moves; solute usually doesn't. In osmosis questions, the solute can't cross. Don't write "the salt moves into the cell."

IDEA 9 · 2.7–2.8

Water potential: a number that tells you where water will go.

In plain English: Water potential (Ψ, "psi") is just a score for how badly water "wants" to leave a place. Pure water scores 0, the highest possible in these problems. Adding solute makes the score negative. Water always flows from the higher score to the lower one: from 0 to −4, or from −2 to −7. That's all Idea 8 said, but now with numbers.

The two parts (both are on the formula sheet: don't memorize, know how to use them)

PieceWhat it isSign
Ψ = Ψs + ΨpTotal water potentialUsually negative
Ψs (solute potential) = −iCRTThe effect of dissolved stuffAlways 0 or negative. More solute → more negative.
Ψp (pressure potential)The push of the cell wall on a swollen plant cell0 in open beakers and in animal cells; positive in turgid plant cells

Ψs = −iCRT, one letter at a time

  • i = how many particles the solute breaks into. Sugars (anything ending in -ose) = 1. NaCl = 2 (Na⁺ + Cl⁻). CaCl₂ = 3. Unit 1 link: ionic bonds ionize in water; each ion gets its own hydration shell, so it ties up more water.
  • C = molar concentration (mol/L).
  • R = 0.0831 L·bar/mol·K (given).
  • T = temperature in kelvin: °C + 273. Forgetting this is the #1 calculation error.

Worked example (same type as her slides, different numbers)

A potato core is placed in an open beaker of 0.2 M sucrose at 22 °C. What is the solute potential of the solution?

i = 1 (sucrose doesn't ionize) · C = 0.2 mol/L · R = 0.0831 · T = 22 + 273 = 295 K
Ψs = −(1)(0.2)(0.0831)(295) = −4.90 bars

Answer in a sentence: "The solute potential of the sucrose solution is −4.90 bars." Include the negative sign, the unit, and a full sentence, with the answer as a decimal. All four of those are on her teacher's list from the first test.

The three problem types

They ask…Do this
"Calculate Ψs"Plug into −iCRT. Kelvin! Sign! Unit!
"Which way will water move?"Find Ψ on both sides (open beaker: Ψp = 0, so Ψ = Ψs). Water goes from higher (less negative) to lower (more negative).
"What is Ψp at equilibrium?"At equilibrium, the cell's Ψ equals the outside Ψ. Set Ψs(cell) + Ψp = Ψ(outside) and solve for Ψp.

Equilibrium example: a cell with Ψs = −6.0 bars is at equilibrium with a solution whose Ψ = −2.5 bars. Then −6.0 + Ψp = −2.5, so Ψp = +3.5 bars. That positive pressure is turgor: the wall pushing back.

Tripping points:
• "Lower" water potential means MORE negative. −7 is lower than −2.
• An open beaker has Ψp = 0. So the solution's Ψ is just its Ψs.
• Pure water: Ψ = 0. Nothing can be higher in these problems, so water always flows out of pure water.
• Same molarity isn't the same Ψs. 0.1 M NaCl pulls twice as hard as 0.1 M sucrose (i = 2 vs. 1).
• Round at the end, as a decimal. Don't leave fractions.

IDEA 10 · 2.8

Pumps build a gradient; other proteins spend it.

In plain English: The cell uses ATP to pump sodium out, so there's always much more Na⁺ outside than inside. Sodium "wants" back in. The cell lets it back in only through special doors that make it carry something else in with it, like glucose. So one ATP-powered pump ends up powering a lot of other transport. That's cotransport (secondary active transport).

The sodium–potassium pump (primary active transport)

Moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, both against their gradients, using ATP. ATP makes the pump change shape, which carries the ions across. Unit 1 link: a protein's shape is its function. Change the shape and you change what it does (conformational change).

"Source of energy?" (her slide asks this): for the symporter, the energy comes from Na⁺ flowing down its gradient, and that gradient was built by the ATP-powered pump. Block the pump and glucose uptake stops, even though the symporter itself never touches ATP.

Proton pumps and the morning-glory flower (her practice problem)

Pumping H⁺ changes pH (Unit 1: more H⁺ = lower pH). In the petals, a K⁺/H⁺ transporter moves H⁺ out of the vacuole (pH rises from 6.6 to 7.7, and the petal turns blue) and K⁺ in. More K⁺ inside means more solute, which means lower water potential, so water rushes in and the vacuole swells. Ideas 8, 9 and 10 in one question.

Tripping points:
• The same ion can move passively in one place and actively in another. Na⁺ enters the cell passively and leaves into the blood actively. That's a real question from her practice set.
• "Active" doesn't always mean ATP directly. Her slide: "always requires energy (not always ATP)." Cotransport uses a gradient.
• Symporter = two things, same direction. Antiporter = opposite directions (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump).

IDEA 11 · 2.9–2.10

Rooms with walls, and where two of the rooms came from.

In plain English: Eukaryotic cells are divided into membrane-bound compartments so that different jobs can happen in different places without getting in each other's way. The compartments came from two sources. Most (nucleus, ER, Golgi) formed when the outer membrane folded inward. Two of them, mitochondria and chloroplasts, were once separate bacteria that got swallowed and stayed (endosymbiosis).

Why compartments help (2.9): her slide gives two reasons

  • Keeps competing reactions apart. Lysosomes hold digestive enzymes that would destroy the cell if they were loose in the cytoplasm. Each compartment can also keep its own conditions (a lysosome is acidic inside).
  • Increases surface area for reactions. The folded inner membrane of the mitochondrion and the ER network. Idea 4 link: more surface area means more exchange and more reactions.

Where the compartments came from (2.10)

OriginWhich organellesHow
Membrane infoldingNucleus (nuclear envelope), ER, Golgi: the endomembrane systemThe plasma membrane of an ancestral prokaryote folded inward around the DNA and into internal membranes
EndosymbiosisMitochondria (from an aerobic bacterium); chloroplasts (from a photosynthetic bacterium)A host cell engulfed the bacterium (endocytosis, Idea 7), didn't digest it, and both benefited
EVIDENCE
Mitochondria and chloroplasts:
1. Double membrane (inner from the bacterium, outer from the engulfing host).
2. Their own DNA, naked and circular, like bacteria.
3. Their own ribosomes, 70S, bacteria-sized (the rest of the cell has 80S).
4. They divide on their own: new ones only come from existing ones, like bacteria.
An FRQ asking you to "explain" the evidence wants the link: "…which matches bacteria, not the eukaryotic nucleus."

Idea 1 link: prokaryotes don't have mitochondria or chloroplasts, but many still do respiration and photosynthesis on folds of their plasma membrane. It's the same surface-area idea, before compartments existed.

Tripping points:
• Two origin stories, different organelles. Infolding explains the nucleus/ER; endosymbiosis explains mitochondria/chloroplasts. Don't mix them.
• Mitochondria came first. Every eukaryote has them; only plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
• "Prokaryotes have no compartments" is too strong. They lack membrane-bound organelles.

Word-pair questions test whether she can tell two related things apart. The winning answer always has two halves: how they're alike, then the one difference that matters, stated for both. Tap a pair only after saying both halves out loud.

Prokaryote & eukaryote
Alike: Both have a plasma membrane, ribosomes, DNA, cytoplasm
Different: Prokaryotes: no nucleus (DNA in a nucleoid), no membrane-bound organelles, smaller. Eukaryotes: nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
Idea 1
Animal cell & plant cell
Alike: Both eukaryotes, both have mitochondria, ER, Golgi
Different: Plants have a cell wall (cellulose), chloroplasts and a large central vacuole; animal cells don't.
Idea 1
Nucleus & nucleolus
Alike: Both are involved with genetic material
Different: Nucleus: double-membrane compartment that holds the DNA. Nucleolus: a region inside the nucleus (no membrane) where ribosomes are made.
Idea 1–2
Cell membrane & cell wall
Alike: Both are boundaries around the cell
Different: Membrane: phospholipid bilayer, selectively permeable, in every cell. Wall: rigid carbohydrate layer outside the membrane (cellulose, chitin, peptidoglycan) for structure; plants, fungi, bacteria only.
Idea 6
Smooth ER & rough ER
Alike: Connected membrane networks
Different: Rough ER has ribosomes and makes proteins to be secreted or put in membranes. Smooth ER has no ribosomes and makes lipids/steroids and detoxifies.
Idea 2
Ribosome & Golgi
Alike: Both are steps in making a protein
Different: Ribosome builds the polypeptide (peptide bonds); no membrane; in all cells. Golgi modifies, packages and ships proteins; membrane-bound; eukaryotes only.
Idea 2
Cilia & flagella
Alike: Both are made of microtubules (9 + 2) and bent by dynein
Different: Cilia: short and many. Flagella: long and few.
Idea 3
Mitochondria & chloroplast
Alike: Both have double membranes, their own DNA and ribosomes, and came from endosymbiosis
Different: Mitochondria: cellular respiration, turning glucose's energy into ATP; in all eukaryotes. Chloroplasts: photosynthesis, capturing light to make sugar; plants and algae only.
Idea 11
Endocytosis & exocytosis
Alike: Both are bulk transport using vesicles and energy
Different: Endo brings material in (vesicle pinches in). Exo sends material out (vesicle fuses with the membrane).
Idea 7
Lysosome & secretory vesicle
Alike: Both are membrane sacs that come from the Golgi
Different: Lysosome keeps hydrolytic enzymes inside to digest things. Secretory vesicle carries a product to the membrane to release it outside (exocytosis).
Idea 2, 11
Integral & peripheral protein
Alike: Both are membrane proteins
Different: Integral: embedded in or spanning the bilayer (nonpolar R-groups in the middle); channels, pumps. Peripheral: attached loosely to the surface.
Idea 5
Receptor, channel, glycoprotein
Alike: All are membrane proteins
Different: Receptor binds a specific signal molecule. Channel makes a passage for specific ions or molecules. Glycoprotein carries a carbohydrate chain used for cell recognition (ID tag).
Idea 5
Phospholipid bilayer & cholesterol
Alike: Both are lipids in the membrane
Different: Bilayer is the barrier itself (amphipathic). Cholesterol is a steroid tucked between phospholipids that buffers fluidity: less fluid when warm, more fluid when cold.
Idea 5
Diffusion & osmosis
Alike: Both are passive, high concentration to low
Different: Diffusion: any substance. Osmosis: specifically water, across a selectively permeable membrane.
Idea 7–8
Passive & active transport
Alike: Both move substances across membranes
Different: Passive: with the gradient (high → low), no energy. Active: against the gradient (low → high), needs energy and a pump.
Idea 7
Simple & facilitated diffusion
Alike: Both passive, high → low, no energy
Different: Simple: straight through the bilayer (small, nonpolar). Facilitated: through a channel or carrier protein (ions, large polar).
Idea 7–8
Solute potential & pressure potential
Alike: Both are parts of water potential, in bars
Different: Ψs comes from dissolved solute; always 0 or negative (−iCRT). Ψp comes from pressure (the cell wall pushing back); 0 in open beakers and animal cells, positive in turgid plant cells.
Idea 9
Ionization constant: sucrose & NaCl
Alike: Both are solutes that lower water potential
Different: Sucrose stays one particle: i = 1. NaCl splits into Na⁺ + Cl⁻: i = 2, so it lowers Ψs twice as much at the same molarity.
Idea 9
Turgid & flaccid
Alike: Both describe plant cells
Different: Turgid: swollen and firm, membrane pressed on the wall (hypotonic surroundings); the healthy state. Flaccid: limp (isotonic surroundings); the plant wilts.
Idea 8
Hypotonic & hypertonic
Alike: Both compare the solute concentrations of two solutions
Different: Hypotonic: less solute (more free water). Hypertonic: more solute. Water moves hypotonic → hypertonic. Always say compared to what.
Idea 8
Dynamic equilibrium & isotonic
Alike: Both involve no net change
Different: Isotonic is the condition: equal solute concentration on both sides. Dynamic equilibrium is what happens then: water keeps moving both ways, but with no net movement.
Idea 8
Endosymbiosis & membrane infolding
Alike: Both explain where eukaryotic compartments came from
Different: Infolding: the plasma membrane folded inward → nucleus, ER, Golgi. Endosymbiosis: an engulfed prokaryote was kept → mitochondria, chloroplasts.

LAB · ACTIVITY A · DIFFUSION (DIALYSIS TUBING)

The question: what can get through the "membrane"?

Setup in one sentence: a bag of dialysis tubing (a model membrane with tiny holes) holds glucose + starch, and sits in a cup of water with IKI (iodine). Two tests tell you where things went: IKI turns blue-black where starch is, and glucose test strips show where glucose is.

How to read the results

If you see…It means…
The inside of the bag turns blue-blackIKI got into the bag (and met the starch)
The cup stays amber (not blue-black)Starch did not get out
The cup tests + for glucoseGlucose got out of the bag

The conclusion the data supports: glucose and IKI are small enough to pass through the pores; starch (a polysaccharide, a huge polymer from Unit 1) is too big. The tubing is selectively permeable by size, like a membrane.

Analysis questions: how to think about each one

  • "Compare your results with your predictions." Go molecule by molecule (water, glucose, starch, IKI): predicted direction, actual evidence, match or not. If a prediction was wrong, say which and why.
  • "Does this account for all the molecules?" Think about which molecule had no test in this activity. What measurement would show where it went? (Hint: something you could weigh.)
  • "What does your data tell you about sizes vs. pore size?" Name each molecule and say whether it's smaller or larger than the pores, citing the color or test-strip evidence.

LAB · ACTIVITY B · POTATO CORE WATER POTENTIAL

The question: what is the water potential inside a potato cell?

The trick behind the whole lab: you can't measure inside a potato cell directly. So you soak potato cores in sugar solutions of different strengths and watch which way water goes. In weak solutions the cores gain mass (water moved in). In strong solutions they lose mass (water moved out). Somewhere in between is a solution where they neither gain nor lose. That solution matches the potato (isotonic), so its concentration tells you the potato's.

Step by step

  1. % change in mass = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. Keep the sign: negative = lost water. Why percent? The cores start at different masses; percent change puts them on the same scale so they can be compared fairly.
  2. Graph the class averages. x-axis (independent variable): sucrose molarity (M). y-axis (dependent variable): % change in mass (%). The y-axis needs negative values. Follow her graphing rules below.
  3. Find where the line crosses 0% on the y-axis (the x-intercept). That molarity is C: the isotonic concentration.
  4. Convert the room temperature to kelvin (°C + 273).
  5. Calculate Ψs = −iCRT with i = 1 (sucrose). Write the answer as a sentence, with the negative sign and bars.

Check her numbers: class potato data usually crosses zero somewhere around 0.2–0.4 M, so Ψs usually comes out around −5 to −10 bars. If she gets a positive number, the sign is missing. If she gets something tiny like −0.02, she forgot to convert to kelvin.

The extension questions: the method, not the answers

  • Chef and vegetables in tap water: compare the Ψ of tap water (close to 0) with the vegetable cells (negative). Water goes from higher to lower. Say what happens to the slices, and why they get crisp.
  • Wheat farm and seawater: you're given two numbers (root Ψs and seawater Ψs). Decide which is higher, which way water would go, and what that does to the plants. Then give the advice, backed by the numbers.
  • Marine clam in fresh water: which is hypotonic compared to the clam's cells? Which way does water move? Clams are animals: is there a wall to stop the cells from swelling?
  • Carrot with corn syrup: the hint asks whether a solution can be hypertonic and hypotonic at the same time. Yes: the carrot cells are hypertonic to the pure water outside but hypotonic to the corn syrup inside. Follow the water from highest Ψ to lowest Ψ. Where does it end up? What happens to the level in the tube?
Tripping points: writing "the potato lost mass because of osmosis" without saying which direction and why (say which side had lower water potential); calling the solution "hypertonic" without "compared to the potato cells"; using the individual group's data when the question asks for class averages.

HER TEACHER'S GRAPHING RULES (FROM THE BLUE SHEET)

A graph point is easy to earn and easy to lose.

  • Independent variable (with units) on the x-axis; dependent variable (with units) on the y-axis. The IV is what you change on purpose. Time is often on the x-axis but is rarely the IV.
  • Simple scale: intervals of 1, 2, 5 or 10. Not 2.5 or 3.
  • Plot every point accurately. One misplaced point loses the plotting point.
  • Connect the dots for line graphs (usually better than a best-fit line). Don't connect to 0,0 unless 0,0 is a data point, and don't extend past the last point unless asked to extrapolate.
  • Never add lines to the graph paper. If the scale is wrong, cross it out and start over.
  • Read the entire FRQ first, and read graphs using a straightedge.

It's an analogy. Say what it actually does: it converts the chemical energy in glucose into ATP.

From 6 to 2. It drops to a third, because SA:V = 6 ÷ side.
At low temperature, what does cholesterol do to a membrane, and how?
It increases fluidity by acting as a spacer that keeps the tails from packing tightly.
Why is the middle of a transmembrane protein made of nonpolar amino acids?
It sits among the nonpolar fatty-acid tails. Like attracts like, so its R-groups there must be nonpolar (hydrophobic).
Rank how easily these cross a bare bilayer: glucose, O₂, Na⁺, water.
O₂ (small, nonpolar) → water (small, polar; a little) → glucose (large, polar; no) and Na⁺ (charged; no). The last two need proteins.
What do all cell walls have in common, and what differs?
All are complex carbohydrates that give structure. They differ in the carbohydrate: cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), peptidoglycan (bacteria).
Why is a folded membrane an advantage? Give two examples.
More surface area means more exchange or more reactions. Examples: microvilli in the intestine, the inner membrane of mitochondria (also the ER).
Simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport: which need a protein, and which need energy?
Simple: neither. Facilitated: a protein (channel/carrier), no energy. Active: a pump protein AND energy.
Explain receptor-mediated endocytosis using only its name.
Endo = into the cell; receptor-mediated = only molecules that bind specific receptor proteins get taken in (inside a vesicle).
A red blood cell goes into distilled water. What happens, and why?
Water moves in (distilled water is hypotonic compared to the cell), and the cell swells and may lyse, because animal cells have no wall.
What's the healthy state for a plant cell? For an animal cell?
Plant: turgid (hypotonic surroundings). Animal: normal in an isotonic solution.
Ψs of 0.1 M NaCl at 25 °C?
−(2)(0.1)(0.0831)(298) = −4.95 bars. (i = 2 because NaCl makes two ions.)
Cell Ψ = −3 bars, solution Ψ = −1 bar. Which way does water move?
From the solution (−1, higher) into the cell (−3, lower).
In the intestine model, Na⁺ enters the cell one way and leaves another. How are they different?
In from the intestine: passive (down its gradient, through the symporter). Out to the blood: active (against its gradient, Na⁺/K⁺ pump, ATP).
What powers the Na⁺/glucose symporter?
The Na⁺ gradient, which the ATP-powered Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains (secondary active transport).

Double membrane; own circular DNA; own 70S ribosomes; they divide on their own like bacteria.
