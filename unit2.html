The big picture

One sentence for the whole unit: a cell is a bag of Unit 1 molecules, divided into compartments, with a membrane that decides what gets in and out.

Every topic in Unit 2 answers one of three questions:

  1. What's inside? The organelles, and what each one does (2.1–2.2).
  2. How big can it be? Surface area to volume (2.2).
  3. What's the wall made of, and what gets through? The membrane (2.3–2.4), and next, how things cross it (2.5–2.8).

IDEA 1 · 2.1

Every living thing is made of cells, and cells come in two basic designs.

In plain English: A cell is the smallest thing that can do everything life does. Some cells are simple, with everything floating together in one open room (prokaryotes: bacteria). Others are divided into rooms with walls, each room with its own job (eukaryotes: plants, animals, fungi). Those "rooms" are organelles, and their walls are membranes.

Cell theory, three parts

All living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the basic unit of life. Cells only come from other cells. The last one is why the teacher says "life is continuous": every cell in you traces back, unbroken, to earlier cells.

Prokaryote vs. eukaryote

Tripping point: "Prokaryotes have no organelles" is a trap answer. They have ribosomes, which count as organelles in many textbooks. What they lack is membrane-bound organelles. When in doubt, say "membrane-bound." Every cell, even the simplest, has a plasma membrane, ribosomes, DNA, and cytoplasm, because every cell has to make proteins.

Plant vs. animal cells

Plants have three things animals don't: a cell wall (cellulose, the β-glucose polymer from Unit 1), chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole. Both have mitochondria. Plants need mitochondria too, because photosynthesis makes sugar but the plant still has to turn that sugar into ATP.

Tripping point: "Plants have chloroplasts instead of mitochondria" is wrong. Plants have both.

Microscopes (one quick fact each)

Light microscope: uses light, sees living cells, limited detail. TEM (transmission electron): electrons pass through a thin slice, so it shows the inside. SEM (scanning electron): electrons bounce off the surface, so it gives a 3-D look at the outside. Memory hook: TEM = Through, SEM = Surface.

IDEA 2 · 2.1–2.2

Follow one protein and you've learned half the organelles.

In plain English: Instead of memorizing a list of organelles, follow a protein from the moment it's made until it leaves the cell. The organelles it passes through in order are the endomembrane system. Once you know the route, each organelle's job is obvious from where it sits on the route.

The route of a secreted protein, like a digestive enzyme

  1. Nucleus: holds the DNA instructions for the protein. Unit 1: nucleic acids store information.
  2. Ribosome on the rough ER: reads the instructions and links amino acids with peptide bonds. Unit 1: dehydration synthesis.
  3. Rough ER: the new protein goes inside, starts folding, and gets its first sugar tags. It's "rough" because it's studded with ribosomes.
  4. Transport vesicle: a small bubble of membrane buds off and carries the protein to the Golgi.
  5. Golgi apparatus: modifies (adds or trims carbohydrates to make glycoproteins), packages and sorts proteins by destination.
  6. Secretory vesicle → plasma membrane: the vesicle fuses with the membrane and releases the protein outside (exocytosis).
ORDER
Nucleus → Ribosome/Rough ER → Vesicle → Golgi → Vesicle → Membrane. The key point: ER before Golgi. The most common mistake is swapping them.

Her own examples: "the Golgi is NOT a stack of pancakes" and "the mitochondria is NOT the powerhouse of the cell."

Instead of: "The mitochondria is the powerhouse of the cell."
Write: "The mitochondrion converts the chemical energy in glucose into ATP through aerobic respiration; its folded inner membrane increases the surface area for these reactions."

IDEA 3 · 2.1

The cytoskeleton is the cell's frame, and its tracks.

In plain English: Inside the cell is a network of protein fibers. Some give it shape, some hold things in place, and some are highways that motor proteins "walk" along, carrying cargo.
FiberMade ofShapeJob
Microtubules ⭐Tubulin (globular protein)Hollow tubes, the thickestTracks for vesicles; move chromosomes during cell division; form cilia and flagella
MicrofilamentsActinSolid thin rodsChange cell shape; muscle contraction
Intermediate filamentsFibrous proteinsRope-likeResist pulling (tension); anchor organelles in place

Cilia and flagella are both built from microtubules in a 9 + 2 pattern (nine pairs in a ring, two in the middle). Cilia are short and many; flagella are long and few.

Motor proteins: dynein bends cilia and flagella by sliding the microtubule pairs; kinesin carries vesicles along microtubules. Unit 1 link: both work by changing shape (a "conformational change"). That's the tertiary structure idea again: a protein's shape is its function, so changing its shape makes it move.

Cell junctions: how neighbor cells connect

JunctionWhat it doesExample
Tight junctionSeals cells together so nothing leaks between themIntestine and bladder lining
DesmosomeRivets cells together; strong, but not sealedSkin, heart muscle
Gap junction ⭐ (animals) / Plasmodesmata ⭐ (plants)Open channels between cells; water, ions, small molecules pass throughHeart cells signaling each other
Tripping point: tight junctions seal; gap junctions connect. If a question mentions "leak-proof," it's tight. If it mentions "communication" or "passing molecules," it's gap junctions (or plasmodesmata in plants).

IDEA 4 · 2.2

Why cells stay small: surface area vs. volume.

In plain English: Everything a cell needs comes in through its surface, and all its waste goes out the same way. The surface is the "doors"; the volume is the "people inside." As a cell grows, the number of people grows much faster than the number of doors. Eventually there aren't enough doors to keep everyone fed. So cells stay small, or fold their surface, or divide.

Watch the numbers (cubes, the way the lab did it)

SideSurface area (6s²)Volume (s³)SA : V
1 cm6 cm²1 cm³6
2 cm24 cm²8 cm³3
4 cm96 cm²64 cm³1.5

Double the size → the SA:V ratio is cut in half. For a cube, SA:V is just 6 ÷ side length, so bigger always means a smaller ratio.

Tripping points:
• "As a cell grows, its surface area decreases." Wrong. The surface area goes up; the ratio goes down. Read carefully for the word "ratio."
• Don't memorize the formulas. They're on the AP formula sheet. Know how to use them, and always include units (cm², cm³).
• A higher SA:V is better for exchange. Small cells win.

The same idea, everywhere in biology (big-picture thread)

Whenever living things need more exchange, they increase surface area: the villi and microvilli of the intestine (absorbing food), the folded inner membrane of mitochondria (more room for reactions), the folded ER, and later root hairs and lungs. You've already seen this in Unit 1: glycogen is highly branched, and more branches means more ends where enzymes can work at once. Same principle.

Why not infinitely small? A cell still has to fit its DNA, ribosomes, and everything needed to carry out life. That sets the lower limit.

IDEA 5 · 2.3

The membrane is Unit 1 chemistry, assembled.

In plain English: A membrane is two layers of phospholipids with proteins floating in it. Water is on both sides of a cell (inside and out), so the water-loving heads face out on both sides and the water-fearing tails hide in the middle. Nobody has to build it: phospholipids arrange themselves this way automatically because of like attracts like.

"Fluid mosaic model," decoded

Fluid: the phospholipids and most proteins drift sideways; the membrane is more like olive oil than plastic wrap. Mosaic: it's made of many different pieces (phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, carbohydrate chains) fitted together.

PartWhat it doesUnit 1 link
PhospholipidsForm the bilayer, the basic barrierAmphipathic: polar head, nonpolar tails
CholesterolKeeps fluidity steady as temperature changesA steroid (four fused rings)
Integral proteinsGo into or all the way through the membrane: channels, transporters, receptorsR-groups decide where they sit

Glycoproteins / glycolipidsCarbohydrate chains on the outside that act as "ID tags" for cell recognitionCarbohydrates + glycosidic bonds

Cholesterol: the "fluidity buffer"

This trips up nearly everyone, because cholesterol does opposite things at different temperatures. Think of it as a buffer, the same idea as pH buffers in Unit 1: it keeps things from swinging too far either way.

TemperatureProblem without cholesterolWhat cholesterol does
WarmMembrane gets too runnyDecreases fluidity (holds the tails back)
ColdMembrane gets too stiff (tails pack tightly)Increases fluidity (acts as a spacer between tails)

Unit 1 link: the cold problem is the saturated-fat problem. Straight tails pack tightly and go solid, like butter. Cholesterol wedges in between them, the same way the kinks of unsaturated fats keep oil liquid.

Membrane proteins: which parts face where

A protein that crosses the membrane has a middle section inside the oily tail layer and ends sticking out into the water on each side. So its middle has nonpolar (hydrophobic) R-groups and its ends have polar (hydrophilic) R-groups. This is the slide 11 "label the protein" question, and it's pure Unit 1.

The phospholipid "orientation" question (slide 9)

SetupWhat forms
Water above, water belowA bilayer: tails face each other in the middle
Oil above, oil belowA reversed bilayer: heads in the middle, tails out toward the oil
Water above, oil belowA single layer: heads up into the water, tails down into the oil

You don't need to memorize these. In every case, heads go toward water and tails go toward oil, and the answer follows.

IDEA 6 · 2.4

What gets through: size and charge decide.

In plain English: The middle of the membrane is oily. Anything that can dissolve in oil (small, nonpolar things) slips right through. Anything charged or large and polar gets stuck and needs a protein doorway. That's what "selectively permeable" means.
Kind of moleculeExamplesCrosses the bilayer by itself?
Small, nonpolarO₂, CO₂, N₂Yes, easily
Large, nonpolarSteroid hormonesYes, slowly
Small, polar, unchargedWater, ureaA little; most water goes through aquaporins (water channels)
Large, polarGlucoseNo: needs a protein
Ions (any size)Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺No: needs a protein
RULE
Charge beats size. A sodium ion is tiny, but it's charged, so it can't cross the oily middle. A steroid is big, but it's nonpolar, so it can. Ask "Is it charged?" first, then "Is it polar?", then "Is it small?"
Tripping points:
• CO₂ is nonpolar, even though its bonds are polar. The two C=O bonds pull equally in opposite directions and cancel out. It crosses easily.
• Water is polar but still crosses, because it's so small. Most of it uses aquaporins, though.
• In the POGIL, urea crossed more easily than glucose. Both are polar; urea is smaller. Size matters once charge is ruled out.

The cell wall

Plants, fungi, and bacteria have a wall outside the plasma membrane. All cell walls are made of complex carbohydrates, and all give structure and help regulate what enters. They differ in which carbohydrate: cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, peptidoglycan in bacteria. Unit 1 link: cellulose is the β-glucose polymer humans can't digest, and chitin is the same material as insect exoskeletons.

Tripping point: the cell wall does not replace the membrane. Plant cells have both; the wall is outside it.

Writing Unit 2 FRQs

Unit 2 FRQs are almost always structure → function. One sentence pattern earns most of the points:

"The [specific structure] [has this feature], which allows it to [do this job]."

Example: "The inner membrane of the mitochondrion is highly folded, which increases its surface area and allows more of the reactions that produce ATP to occur."
  • Name the actual structure. "The rough ER," not "it" or "that organelle."
  • No analogies. They score zero (her rule).
  • Describe = what it is. Explain = how or why. "Describe the path of a protein" wants the steps in order. "Explain why the protein can't leave without the Golgi" wants the reason.
  • For "predict" questions about a cell type, match organelles to the job (the table in Idea 2).
  • For SA:V calculations, show the setup, and include units on every number.
  • Connect to Unit 1 when it fits. "Nonpolar R-groups face the hydrophobic tails" shows understanding and often earns the explanation point.

Self-check

Answer out loud before tapping. Hesitating counts as a miss; mark it and come back tomorrow.

What do ALL cells have, even bacteria?
A plasma membrane, ribosomes, DNA, and cytoplasm. Every cell has to make proteins, and ribosomes do that.
Put in order: Golgi, rough ER, secretory vesicle, ribosome, transport vesicle.
Ribosome (on the rough ER) → rough ER → transport vesicle → Golgi → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane.
A cell makes lots of steroid hormone. Which organelle will be abundant, and why?
Smooth ER: steroids are lipids, and lipid synthesis happens in the smooth ER.
Lysosomes use "hydrolytic" enzymes. What does that word tell you they do?
They break polymers apart by adding water (hydrolysis, from Unit 1).
Why is "the mitochondria is the powerhouse of the cell" worth zero points?
It's an analogy. Say what it actually does: it converts the chemical energy in glucose into ATP.
Tight junction or gap junction: which lets small molecules pass between cells?
Gap junction (plasmodesmata in plants). Tight junctions seal.
A cube goes from 1 cm to 3 cm on a side. What happens to its SA:V ratio?
From 6 to 2. It drops to a third, because SA:V = 6 ÷ side.
At low temperature, what does cholesterol do to a membrane, and how?
It increases fluidity by acting as a spacer that keeps the tails from packing tightly.
Why is the middle of a transmembrane protein made of nonpolar amino acids?
It sits among the nonpolar fatty-acid tails. Like attracts like, so its R-groups there must be nonpolar (hydrophobic).
Rank how easily these cross a bare bilayer: glucose, O₂, Na⁺, water.
O₂ (small, nonpolar) → water (small, polar; a little) → glucose (large, polar; no) and Na⁺ (charged; no). The last two need proteins.
What do all cell walls have in common, and what differs?
All are complex carbohydrates that give structure. They differ in the carbohydrate: cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), peptidoglycan (bacteria).
Why is a folded membrane an advantage? Give two examples.
More surface area means more exchange or more reactions. Examples: microvilli in the intestine, the inner membrane of mitochondria (also the ER).
